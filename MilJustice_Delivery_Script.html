Overview of Military Justice

EditedGood morning. For the next forty minutes we're covering military justice. You will be tested on this, forty minutes, multiple choice, seventy percent to pass, and I'll send you a study guide when we're done.

But passing the test is the small reason to pay attention. Here's the real one. Every single one of you is about to be in a position where a Soldier does something wrong and people turn and look at you. What you're about to see is the complete menu of what you're allowed to do about it.

Administrative separation. Nonjudicial punishment. Court-martial. Your job as a leader is picking the right rung. Not the highest one, not the easiest one. The right one.

2

Why we have a military justice system

0:45

Four purposes. Promote justice. Maintain good order and discipline. Promote efficiency and effectiveness. Strengthen national security.

Look at that list carefully. Justice is one out of four. A civilian court exists to do justice, and that's the end of the mission statement. Ours has to do justice and keep a fighting force functional at the same time.

That's not a small difference. It's the reason we have crimes that don't exist anywhere else in American law, and it's the reason a commander has options that a civilian prosecutor doesn't have. Hold onto that, because it explains most of what comes next.

UCMJ jurisdiction

1:30

Jurisdiction runs two directions. Over the person, and over the offense.

Over the person. Active duty Soldiers, and Reservists while on active duty. Military academy cadets and midshipmen. National Guard personnel in Federal service, under Title 10. Retirees. Enemy prisoners of war. And in some cases, civilians deployed with units.

Two of those have qualifiers on them and I want you to catch both. Reservists, while on active duty. Not all the time. And National Guard personnel in Federal service, Title 10.

EditedThink about your own status. In state status, under Title 32, you're under your state's code of military justice. Not this one. The moment you're federalized, everything on this slide applies to you personally. That distinction is on the exam, and it's also just a true fact about your career.

Retirees are on that list too, and yes, that surprises people. So are enemy prisoners of war.

Over the offense, two things. Worldwide jurisdiction, which means the UCMJ follows you. There's no getting out from under it by being off post or in another country. And possible concurrent jurisdiction, which means civilian authorities may have a claim on the exact same conduct at the same time. Both systems can want the same Soldier.

Crimes under the UCMJ

3:30

Two columns here.

Common law crimes. These are the ones any civilian would recognize. Homicide. Rape. Larceny. Robbery. Assault. Burglary. Crimes anywhere in the country.

Military crimes. These have no civilian equivalent at all. Disrespect. Disobedience. Absence without leave. Conduct unbecoming an officer. Conduct prejudicial to good order and discipline.

Here's the difference in one example. If you work at a civilian job and you tell your boss exactly what you think of him and walk out the door, you are unemployed. You are not arrested. Nobody charges you. In this organization, that's disrespect and disobedience, and both are crimes.

For the exam, expect to be handed an offense and asked which column it belongs in.

Article 15 responsibilities

8:50

Before imposing an Article 15, a commander should inquire about three things. Whether an offense was committed. Whether the Soldier was involved. And the character and military record of the Soldier.

Counseling Soldiers

16

Corrective training

16:45

Corrective training, also called remedial training. Four rules.

It has to be directly related to the deficiency you observed and oriented to correcting that specific deficiency. It is training, not punishment. It must not be humiliating or degrading. And a leader should be present.

Let me make that concrete.

Soldier is consistently late. You put him on bi-hourly check-ins until he demonstrates he can be where he's supposed to be, when he's supposed to be there. That's related to the deficiency. It corrects the deficiency. It's defensible all day long.

Same Soldier, same lateness, and instead you have him fill sandbags for two hours. Ask yourself what deficiency that corrects. It doesn't correct anything. So now it isn't training, it's punishment. And you just imposed punishment without any of the process that punishment requires, which means you have a problem and your commander has a problem.

The test is always the same single question. Does this train the thing that was deficient? If you can't answer that cleanly, don't do it.

And on the leader being present. If nobody's watching, it isn't training. It's just something unpleasant you assigned.

Characterization of service

21:45

Three characterizations.

Honorable. No misconduct, and the Soldier is entitled to all benefits.

General. Minor misconduct, entitled to most benefits.

Other Than Honorable. More serious misconduct, requires a board, and the Soldier forfeits most benefits.

Inspections

33:00

Do not cut

An inspection is not a search. That's the headline.

The primary purpose must be to ensure the security, military fitness, and good order and discipline of the unit. And because it isn't a search, it does not require probable cause.

Here's the example. Health and welfare inspection of the barracks. No probable cause, no authorization from any of the three people on the earlier slide, and it is completely lawful. The purpose is unit readiness.

Now watch those two words, primary purpose. Say you already suspect one specific Soldier of one specific thing, and you call a health and welfare on the entire barracks so you can get into his room. Your primary purpose isn't readiness anymore. That's a search. And you just conducted it without probable cause and without authorization.

Same physical act, in the same building, on the same day. Different intent. Completely different legal outcome. That's why this distinction matters.
