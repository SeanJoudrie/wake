# PLACE NAMES
*in the charting office it was the name book*
`INTACT`

A clerk in the Polaris charting office spent eleven years writing letters to settlements to ask one question, which was where the name came from. Nine thousand seven hundred and fourteen of them wrote back.

**Full title:** *Origins of Registered Settlement Names, Fourth Revision*
**Compiled:** GC 4352 to 4363. Revised GC 4370
**Held:** one bound copy. Water damage at the spine, 91 pages affected
**Names listed:** 9,714, on 190 bodies
**Origins recorded:** 6,208
**Names given two origins and printed with both:** 1,046
**Correspondents named in the acknowledgements:** 2,918
**Print run:** 400. Copies traced: 1

The method was correspondence and nothing else. The clerk wrote to rate offices, port masters, schoolteachers and settlement councils, asked where the name came from, printed what came back, and where two answers came back that could not both be right printed them one after the other and adjudicated nothing.

The counts below are the clerk's own, taken from the summary sheet, which is undamaged. Each name is counted once, under the origin listed first.

- Named for a person, 2,411
- Carried in from another settlement, 1,388
- Taken from the people already on the ground, 802
- Descriptive of the place, 689
- Issued by a company, 581
- Given before there was a settlement, 337
- No origin recorded, 3,506

**Named for whoever filed the paperwork.** The largest class by a distance. Survey contractors, claim agents, company factors, the man who paid for the first landing field and the man who signed the instrument authorising him to. The volume gives the profession of the person in 2,388 of these.

It records a person who was living on the ground before the filing in eleven.

**Names carried in from somewhere else.** A settlement takes the name of the place its founders left, and a settlement founded out of that one takes it again, and the name walks outward from a world nobody in the fourth town has seen. The clerk printed these as chains. The longest runs to six links and ends on a body that is now uninhabited.

Every chain in the volume is commercial, because at the date of compilation that was the only reason anyone moved. Every chain the Restoration has been able to extend past GC 4401 is a displacement.

**Names taken from the people who were there first.** Written into Galactic Standard by surveyors who did not speak what they were writing down, from whoever was standing at the landing site, and glossed with the meaning that person gave. Where the volume carries two glosses they are frequently not near each other. One name on Corr is given as *the good water* and as *the water we were told about*, on the same page, from two correspondents forty kilometres apart.

**Descriptive names.** The ground, the weather, the water, the smell. These are the names most likely to be accurate and the names most often replaced, because a world seeking investment does not keep a name that means the dust.

**Company names.** A depot number people went on using. A lot code. A product. A berth designation that hardened. Where the company is gone the name is generally the last surviving evidence that it was ever there. An appendix carries a further 212 named features with no settlement on them, of which J.LOT is one. The letter is not explained.

**Names given before there was anything there.** Named by whoever was selling the ground. For this class the volume holds both the date of the name and the date of the first resident, and the first is earlier than the second by a median of four years.

**No origin recorded.** More than a third of the volume. Either nobody replied, or the reply was that nobody knew. The clerk left the line after the name empty rather than close it.

**The name that repeats.** *Landing* appears 61 times. The second most repeated name in the volume is a surname, 44 times, on 44 bodies, and each of those 44 settlements answered separately that it was named for a man who had filed there. The volume does not connect them. The surviving claim registers do. Pellam Drost was a claim agent, and he never lived on any of them.

*[Compiler's note: the acknowledgements run to 2,918 names, each printed with the settlement the writer answered from. It is the largest list of ordinary people, named and located, that we hold. It was assembled in order to source place names and it is the closest thing to a census that survived.]*
